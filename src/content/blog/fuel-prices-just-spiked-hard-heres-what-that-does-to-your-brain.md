## The pump just got a lot more painful

BBC News reports that diesel prices in the UK have hit an all-time high of 199.18p per litre, according to the RAC. The RAC says prices have entered "uncharted territory," driven by the ongoing war in the Middle East.

That's a big number. And if you drive to clients, retreats, workshops, or anywhere your work takes you, you felt it the moment you stood at the pump.

But here's the thing I want to talk about. It's not just your wallet that's hurting. It's your nervous system.

---

## Why this feels bigger than it is

There's a well-established idea in behavioural economics called loss aversion. Kahneman and Tversky identified it: losses feel roughly twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good. So a cost going up hits harder, emotionally, than a revenue win of the same size would feel rewarding.

When diesel rises by 59p a litre, as BBC News reports it has since the conflict began at the end of February, you don't just register a cost increase. Your brain registers a loss. Something was taken. Something you had is now gone.

That's not weakness. That's just how human brains are built.

---

## Then the feast-or-famine loop kicks in

For self-employed people, coaches, and wellness practitioners, loss aversion rarely stays quiet. It tends to echo.

You fill the tank. You see the number. Your brain logs a loss. Then it starts scanning for other losses. What else is going up? What if bookings drop? What if clients can't afford me anymore? What if I can't afford to keep driving to them?

That spiral has a name too. It connects to what Brad and Ted Klontz call money scripts, the deep, often unconscious stories we carry about money and safety. For a lot of self-employed people, one of those scripts sounds like: my income is always one shock away from collapse.

A fuel spike doesn't create that belief. It just wakes it up.

---

## There's also something called the ostrich effect

Galai and Sade identified the ostrich effect: when financial information feels threatening, people avoid looking at it altogether. You know the tank needs filling, but you don't want to check the price. You know your running costs have gone up, but you don't open the spreadsheet.

This makes total sense as a short-term coping move. But it means you're making decisions, about pricing, about bookings, about how many client visits you say yes to, without the actual numbers in front of you.

The avoidance feels like relief. It's actually just delayed discomfort, usually with interest.

---

## One small thing to do this week

Here's the action. This week, just write down your actual monthly driving costs as they stand right now. Not to fix anything. Not to panic. Just to look.

When you put a real number on paper, it stops being a vague threat. Your brain stops catastrophising because it has something concrete to work with. The fog lifts a little.

BBC News reports that the RAC's Simon Williams said these increased costs "will be passed on to consumers." That's worth sitting with too. You are not the only person absorbing this. The whole supply chain is adjusting. That doesn't make it easy, but it does mean you're not uniquely exposed.
