**What you need to know**
- A traditional monthly budget assumes a steady wage. If your income swings, you need a floor, not a forecast.
- Mental accounting (Thaler) and earmarking (Soman and Cheema) are the two behavioural tools that make variable income manageable.
- Know your minimum number. When you do, a lean month stops feeling like a crisis.

---

If you've never drawn a consistent salary because your income has never been consistent enough, a standard budget template is going to feel almost offensive. It asks you to fill in a monthly income figure when you genuinely don't know what that number will be. So you leave it blank, close the tab, and tell yourself you'll sort it when things settle down.

That hum of money panic in the background, the one that's just always there, it doesn't go away. It gets louder in a lean month and quieter in a good one. But it doesn't stop.

So people come to budgeting with the wrong tool. They're trying to plan forward from an unknown number, and that's genuinely hard. The way through is to plan backward from a number you do know.

This post gives you that number and shows you what to do with it.

---

## The quick answer

Work out the minimum your household needs to run each month, then build a system around protecting that number, not predicting your income.

---

## What you might be telling yourself

**"My income is all over the place, so budgeting just doesn't apply to me."**
Budgeting for variable income works differently. The floor matters more than the forecast.

**"This last month is lean. I'm telling you, I just don't have it."**
A lean month is information, not a character flaw. It shows you where the floor actually sits, and that's the number to plan around.

**"I've been floundering as to how to pay myself. I don't even know where to start."**
Most people in business skip this step. Your wage is a line item, a real cost, the same as any other bill the business pays.

**"The money is gone and I don't even know where it went."**
That feeling usually comes from money sitting in one undivided pile. When it's all in one place, it all looks available, so it all gets spent.

**"I dread tax season every year. It always catches me off guard."**
Tax doesn't arrive unexpectedly. It builds quietly while you're looking the other way. You'll see how to deal with that below.

**"I feel like I'm in survival mode no matter how much I bring in."**
More income alone doesn't fix that feeling. The system around the income is what changes it.

## Why this hits self-employed people harder

If you work for someone else, a payroll system does the boring structural work for you. Tax comes out before you see the money. Your take-home is the same every month. You know what you're working with.

When you run your own thing, money arrives in lumps. One client pays late. A big project lands in the same week as two smaller ones. Then nothing for three weeks. Nobody runs a payroll for you. Tax waits at the end of the year like a quiet bill you forgot you were running up. Business and personal money blur together, especially if you're paying household bills from the business account some months.

The standard budget was built for a world where none of that happens. That's why it doesn't work for you, not because you're bad at money.

## A scene you'll recognise

It's a good month. The account looks healthy. So you pay the bills, buy the thing you've been putting off, take on a new tool you've been meaning to try. Then a slow month comes. And now the account that looked fine two weeks ago is suddenly a problem.

You didn't blow it. You just didn't have a system that told you which part of that money was already spoken for.

## What's actually happening to the money once it lands

This is the part most people never see. When income arrives in one lump, your brain reads the whole thing as available. Richard Thaler's work on mental accounting shows we file money into mental pots, and those pots change how we spend it. The problem is, when there's only one pot, everything looks like spending money.

Dilip Soman and Amar Cheema's research on earmarking showed that physically separating money (even just labelling it) makes people less likely to spend it on the wrong thing. The pot changes the behaviour.

So the moment income lands, it needs to be split. Not later. Not when you feel like it. Right then.

Here's the basic shape:

- A pot for tax. When a payment arrives, move a portion across immediately. This is not your money. Treat it like it arrived already deducted. A qualified accountant in your country can help you work out what portion makes sense for your situation.
- A pot for business costs.
- A pot for your wage. A fixed amount you pay yourself, even in a slow month.
- A buffer. Anything beyond those three, that's what grows the buffer for the lean months.

You don't need four different banks to do this. Even labelled savings pots inside one account work. The label changes the psychology.

## The behavioural ideas behind it

**Mental accounting (Thaler):** we treat money differently depending on where it sits and how we label it. One pool feels like one big available amount. Four labelled pots feel like four different things, which is exactly what they are.

**Earmarking (Soman and Cheema):** when money is visibly set aside for a specific purpose, we're less likely to touch it for something else. The physical act of moving it, even a few seconds on a banking app, changes how we see it.

**Loss aversion (Kahneman and Tversky):** losing what's already in a pot hurts more than the equivalent gain would feel good. So once your tax pot has money in it, you'll feel more reluctant to raid it. That friction is useful. Let it work for you.

**Money scripts (Klontz):** beliefs like "I'll never have enough" or "good months don't last" drive behaviour before you're even aware of it. If survival mode is your default setting regardless of the income, that's worth looking at. The system helps. The belief work helps too.

## One thing to try today

Write down your floor.

Not what you'd like to earn. Not your best month. Your minimum number: the total your household genuinely needs to keep running for one month. Rent or mortgage. Food. Utilities. Insurance. The basics.

Write that number on paper before you open any app. Then ask: is my current wage to myself covering that number? That's the only question that matters right now. Everything else builds from there.

## Questions people ask

### How do I budget when I don't have a regular income?

Start with your floor, the minimum your household needs each month, rather than trying to forecast income. Then earmark money into separate pots the moment it arrives so tax, costs, your wage and a buffer are all visible and separate.

### How do I pay myself when my business income changes every month?

Set a fixed amount as your wage, even if it's lower than you'd like in a slow month. Paying yourself a consistent amount protects your household and makes the business treat your wage as a real cost, which it is. Adjust the amount quarterly if you need to, but keep it consistent month to month.

### Why do I always feel behind even in a good month?

Often it's because all the money sits in one place and looks available, so it gets spent on everything at once. When a good month's income isn't split into labelled pots immediately, it disappears into general spending before tax, buffer or your own wage gets a look in.

### Should I set money aside for tax every month?

Move a portion aside every time income lands, not monthly. Waiting until the end of the month means you may have already spent it. The exact portion depends on your income, your country's tax rules and your business structure, so a qualified accountant is the right person to help you land on a number.

### What if my income is so low some months that I can't fill any of the pots?

Then the buffer pot is the priority. In a good month, you're not just covering that month, you're partly covering the lean one that's coming. The buffer exists so a slow month doesn't empty the other pots. That's its only job.
