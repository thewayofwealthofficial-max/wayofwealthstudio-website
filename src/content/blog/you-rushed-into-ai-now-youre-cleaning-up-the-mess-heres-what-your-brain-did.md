## The AI rush has a hangover

The Guardian reports that employers around the globe are now dealing with the fallout from rushing to adopt AI tools too fast. We're talking errors, low-quality work, security risks, and costs that keep climbing. Some companies are pulling back, limiting usage, and bringing experienced workers in again.

So yeah. The thing that was supposed to save time is eating time.

And if you're a freelancer, consultant, or creative, this story lands right on your doorstep.

---

## Why this matters to you specifically

You probably felt the pressure. AI felt urgent, like you'd get left behind if you didn't jump in. Maybe you did jump in. Maybe you're still figuring out what actually helps and what just creates more work.

Now the big companies are finding the same thing. A Microsoft executive, The Guardian reports, publicly warned colleagues about a potential "doom loop" where AI does the reading and the writing and leaves behind an "ocean of sameness." The company also found that "adding agents to a broken process still leaves a broken process."

That's not just a corporate problem. That's a you problem too, right?

---

## This has a name in behavioural economics: present bias

Present bias is the tendency to overweight what feels good or useful right now and underweight the costs that land later.

Think of it like this. You're holding two options. One hand has a shiny tool that looks like it'll save you hours today. The other hand has a slower, messier version of the same result that takes more thought up front. Present bias says your brain will reach for the shiny hand almost every time, because the future costs feel abstract and the present gain feels real and immediate.

It's not weakness. It's how we're wired.

Economists have known about this pattern for a long time. The research on it is solid. We consistently choose the smaller, sooner reward over the larger, later one, even when we'd tell you the opposite if you asked us in advance.

---

## The hidden cost problem

Here's the thing about AI adoption, and honestly about a lot of decisions you make in your business. The upfront cost is visible. You pay for the tool, you spend a few hours learning it, done. But the downstream costs are invisible until they're not.

The Guardian reports that an Ernst & Young survey found that 36% of senior AI executives at companies with at least $1bn in annual revenue reported an AI incident that led to data loss, financial damage, brand damage or operational disruptions. These aren't small businesses guessing. These are well-resourced organisations with whole teams, and they still didn't see the cost clearly until it had already landed.

For you, the hidden cost might look different. It could be client work that needs more rounds of revision because the first draft was "plausible-looking" but wrong, which is how Jessica Reif, assistant professor at the Wharton School, describes AI output in The Guardian piece. It could be your own thinking going a bit thin because you outsourced the part that used to sharpen you. It could just be the quiet cost of spending time managing the tool instead of doing the work.

Present bias didn't show you any of that at the start. It showed you the save.

---

## This is not an argument against AI

I want to be clear about that. Using tools is fine. Being thoughtful about which ones and how is the point.

What present bias does is push you to decide fast and count the costs later. The antidote isn't to never adopt anything new. It's to slow the decision down enough to ask: what does this actually cost, not just today, but in a month when I'm deeper in?

The Society for Human Resource Management president is quoted in The Guardian saying that with AI, "we're driving the truck and building it at the same time, and that's a problem." He added that, looking back, they would have introduced it "in a more thoughtful, slow way."

Thoughtful and slow doesn't mean passive. It just means present bias doesn't get to make the call on its own.

---

## One small thing for this week

Before you add any new tool, subscription, or system to your work, write down three costs that won't show up until later. Not money costs necessarily. Time, attention, quality, the mental load of managing one more thing. Just name them. That's it.

Writing them down makes the future feel more real to your brain. It's a simple way to rebalance what present bias tips out of whack.
