## The shame that shows up at the door with your food

The Guardian reports that half of Gen Z respondents in a recent survey said they felt embarrassed about how much they spend on food delivery, sometimes so much that they lie about it to friends and family.

One person quoted in the piece put it plainly: "Nine times out of 10, I feel bad about myself."

So. If you read that and felt a little seen, you're not broken. You're doing something your brain is wired to do.

And honestly, the delivery guilt is just the most visible version of something I see in self-employed people all the time.

## Present bias: now beats later, every time

There's a name for what's happening. It's called present bias. And it's not a character flaw. It's a feature of how the human brain works.

Present bias means that right now, this moment, carries enormous weight in our decisions, and the future shrinks down to almost nothing by comparison. Not because we don't care about future us. But because future us feels like a stranger.

You know that feeling when you're tired, it's been a hard week, you don't want to cook, and the app is right there? That's not laziness. That's present you making a perfectly rational trade: comfort now, cost later.

And then the order arrives. And delivery guilt walks in with it.

Now, here's where it gets interesting for you as a self-employed person. The food delivery thing is just one example of this pattern. Think about the money admin you're going to sort next month. The invoice you'll chase when you have more energy. The tax you'll deal with when things calm down. That's all present bias, just wearing different clothes.

## The guilt loop that keeps the habit going

The Guardian quotes Joy Lu, an assistant professor of marketing at Carnegie Mellon University who studies delivery app pricing. She says people might not even realise how much they are spending on delivery, or consciously avoid looking into the cost. Her words: "It's very easy to spend a bit at a time."

That avoiding is its own thing. It's what Dan Galai and Orly Sade called the ostrich effect: the way we bury our heads and avoid financial information that might be uncomfortable. Because looking feels worse than not looking, in the short term.

So the loop goes: order for comfort, feel guilty, feel shame about the amount, avoid looking at the total, order again for comfort.

Right?

And when your income is unpredictable, like it is for most of the people I work with, the loop gets tighter. A good month feels like permission to spend. A bad month feels like punishment. Neither of those feelings is a plan.

## Why this isn't really about the food

Present bias isn't something you switch off. You can't just decide to stop feeling the pull of right now. What you can do is change what right now looks like.

Richard Thaler's work on mental accounting tells us that we make completely different decisions about money depending on which mental pot it's sitting in. The money on the app feels separate from the money in your business account, which feels separate from the money you owe in tax. But it isn't. It's all the same money. And present you is the one spending the pot that future you needed.

None of this is about judging the delivery order. Genuinely. The Guardian piece quotes someone describing food delivery as an "affordable luxury", a way to treat yourself without a big outlay. That makes sense. Small treats are not the enemy.

The chaos underneath is the thing worth looking at.

## One small thing for this week

Not a big overhaul. Just this.

Before you place your next order, whatever it is, spending on the app, putting off a money task, skipping the invoice, take about ten seconds and name what present you is choosing and what it costs future you. Not to stop yourself. Just to notice it.

That's it. Awareness first. Because you can't change a pattern you haven't seen clearly yet.

The gap between the impulse and the action is where everything happens. And you build that gap by noticing, not by punishing yourself for the order you already placed.
