**What you need to know**
- The urge to spend everything is a pattern you learned, often a long time ago, and it can be changed.
- For self-employed people, irregular income makes this pattern worse, not better.
- One small change to what you do the moment money lands can shift the whole thing.

---

If you run your own business and money lands in your account, you spend it, and then you wonder where it went, you are not alone. A lot of people in service businesses describe the same thing. A good month comes in, and somehow it's gone by the time the next one arrives.

It can feel like something is wrong with you. Like everyone else has some self-control you were never given. But that framing isn't useful. So let's look at what's actually happening.

This post explains why the urge to spend everything feels so urgent, what's driving it under the surface, and what you can do about it today.

## The quick answer

The feeling that you have to spend all your money usually comes from a deep, learned belief that money won't stay, so you might as well use it now. That belief made sense at some point. It doesn't have to run the show forever.

## What you might be telling yourself

**"The money's gone and I don't even know where."**
When money moves fast and there's no system in place to show it where to go first, that's exactly what happens.

**"We make a good living but it's so stressful."**
Earning well and feeling stable are two different things. Income without structure can feel just as panicky as not enough income.

**"The more I make, the more I seem to owe."**
When spending rises to meet income automatically, it's called lifestyle creep. Nobody plans it. It just happens when there's no gap between money arriving and money being used.

**"I've been blowing all of it and not even leaving enough for taxes."**
That's incredibly common for self-employed people. It's also one of the most fixable things, once you know why it's happening.

**"I feel like I'm always in survival mode."**
That hum of money panic in the background is real. And it can actually make the spending worse, not better. More on that in a second.

**"I've never drawn a proper salary because the income's never been consistent."**
So every time money arrives, it doesn't feel like income. It feels like relief. And relief gets spent.

## Why this hits harder when you run your own business

When you work for someone else, your salary hits the same day every month. You know it's coming. Your brain can plan around it.

When you're self-employed, money comes in lumps. A big project lands, or three invoices clear at once, and suddenly there's more in the account than there was last week. Then a slow month, and it's gone.

Nobody pays you a wage. Nobody moves the tax aside. Nobody separates what the business needs from what you need. All of that lands on you, and if you don't have a system, money just pools together and then drains.

Business and personal money often get tangled too, especially early on. Some months you might even need to empty the business account to cover the household bills. That makes everything feel precarious, even in a good month.

So when money arrives, there's this pull to use it, right now, before something takes it. That pull is your nervous system talking, not your logic brain.

## A scene you might recognise

A decent-sized payment lands. You feel a wave of relief. You pay the thing you've been putting off, treat yourself to something small because you've been working so hard, sort a few household bills, and before you've really thought about it, the balance is back to where it always is.

No single thing was wild. But the money is gone.

And the slow month is two weeks away.

## What happens to the money once it arrives

Here's the part most people never see. When money lands and there's no instruction for it, it goes to the most urgent thing in front of you. That feels efficient. It isn't.

The tax bill isn't urgent yet, so nothing goes there. The buffer doesn't feel urgent either, so nothing goes there. Your own pay might not even feel real if you've never set it up properly, so nothing goes there either.

What's left is everything else, and it expands to fill the space.

Give the money a destination before your hands get to it. Even a few minutes after it lands, before you open the banking app properly, before you start paying things, decide what goes where. A pot for tax (moved the moment an invoice clears), a pot for your own steady pay, a buffer for the lean months. What's left after that, you can use freely.

For a closer look at building a structure for variable income, [How do I budget when my income is different every month?](/blog/how-do-i-budget-when-my-income-is-different-every-month/) goes into that in more detail.

## The behavioural ideas behind it

A few things are working on you at once here, and they're worth naming.

**Money scripts** (coined by Brad Klontz and Ted Klontz in 2011) are the unconscious beliefs about money we formed early in life. They run in the background and can feel like just "who you are," so we rarely think to question them. Money avoidance is one of the patterns Klontz describes: a belief that money is dangerous, scarce, or temporary. If you grew up hearing that money doesn't last, or watching it disappear, you can end up with a script that says spend it before it goes. You're not reckless. You're following a rule you didn't choose.

**Scarcity mindset** builds on this. When your income is unpredictable, you can get locked into a scarcity loop, treating every amount as "not enough" even when the number in the account says otherwise. Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir's work on scarcity shows how the feeling of not having enough captures mental bandwidth and changes the decisions we make, often making the problem worse.

**Present bias** is the tendency to weight what's in front of us right now much more heavily than what's coming later. Thaler and Sunstein's work on this explains a lot. The tax bill in January feels abstract compared to the electricity bill today. So today wins. Every time.

And **mental accounting**, which Thaler identified, means we treat money differently depending on where it came from or how we're thinking about it. A big payment landing can feel like "extra" money, which gets spent differently to the regular kind. It's the same money. But the label changes the behaviour.

## One small step to try today

Write the number down before you open the app. Literally, before you check the balance, write down: what does tax need from this? What does my own wage need from this? What does the buffer need?

Even if the numbers are rough. Even if you can't move anything yet. The act of deciding before you see the money puts you in a different relationship with it. You're not reacting. You're directing.

That one move, done consistently, starts to break the pattern.

## Questions people ask

### Why do I spend all my money even when I earn well?
Earning well and having a system for where money goes are two different things. Without a system, money lands and flows straight to the most visible needs. The urge to spend everything often comes from a scarcity-driven money script, a deep belief that money won't stay, which drives you to use it before it disappears.

### Is spending all your money a scarcity mindset?
Scarcity mindset can definitely drive it. When your income is irregular, you can stay locked in "not enough" mode even when the balance looks fine. Mullainathan and Shafir's research on scarcity shows how that feeling narrows focus and pulls decisions toward the short term.

### Why do I always feel behind even in a good month?
Good months can feel just as stressful as bad ones when money arrives without direction. It lands, it moves fast, and by the time the next slow month comes, the buffer isn't there. What looks like a spending problem is often a timing and structure problem.

### What is present bias and why does it make me spend everything now?
Present bias is the tendency to heavily favour immediate needs over future ones. A tax bill due in six months feels far less real than a subscription renewing today, so the future bill keeps losing. Thaler and Sunstein's work on present bias explains why this happens and why giving money a destination the moment it lands helps bypass it.

### How do I stop feeling like money is flying out without me knowing where it goes?
Start by tracking where it went in the last month, not to judge yourself, but to get the information. Then the next time a payment lands, before you do anything else, write down what that money needs to cover: tax, your own pay, a buffer. That small pause between money arriving and money moving is where the pattern starts to change.
