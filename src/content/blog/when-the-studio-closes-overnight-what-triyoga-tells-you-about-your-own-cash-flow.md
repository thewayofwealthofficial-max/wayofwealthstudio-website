## This one hit close to home

So, The Guardian reports that Triyoga, a well-known London yoga studio chain, shut without warning in September, leaving an estimated 100 teachers unpaid for their final weeks of work.

One teacher put her loss at about £1,500. Another is reportedly facing homelessness. These are not people who were careless or foolish. These are working professionals who trusted a system, showed up, taught their classes, and got nothing back.

And honestly, if you work in wellness, coaching, or any kind of bodywork, this story probably landed somewhere in your chest. Because you know how close to the edge this life can run.

## The mental trap that made this worse

Here's the behavioural idea I want to name properly: mental accounting.

Richard Thaler, who won the Nobel Prize for this work, showed that we don't treat all money the same way in our heads. We put it into separate mental buckets. Money we've already earned feels different from money we're still owed. Money sitting in our account feels real. Money that's coming next week feels almost as real, because we've already done the work.

That's the trap. You teach the class, so the money feels like yours. You start spending against it in your head, sometimes before it even arrives. You plan next month's rent, or you think you can absorb a slow week, because that studio pay is coming. Right?

Except when it doesn't come, there's no buffer. The mental bucket was full. The actual account was not.

## Why freelancers are especially exposed

The Guardian quotes Davy Jones, chair of the Yoga Teachers' Union, who said this is "a wake-up call for yoga teachers to realise that they are actually part of that same gig economy" as Uber and Deliveroo drivers.

That framing matters. Because when you work for a studio, it can feel like something more stable than gig work. There's a relationship. There's a schedule. There's a community, like Robin Catto, who taught at Triyoga for 26 years, described. That warmth is real. But the financial structure underneath it can be just as fragile as any platform job.

And mental accounting makes this worse in a specific way. We tend to underweight the risk on income that feels regular. If a studio pays you every month, your brain starts filing that under reliable, even when the actual security underneath is thin. The feeling of regularity replaces actual stability in your mental model. That's not a character flaw. It's just how we're wired.

Kahneman and Tversky's work on loss aversion adds another layer here. Losing income you've already earned, money you've mentally spent, hits about twice as hard as missing out on a bonus you never expected. The teachers in this story aren't just losing money. They're losing money they already counted. That's a different kind of pain, and the research backs up why it feels so crushing.

## There's also something called the ostrich effect

Galai and Sade identified this one. When we sense that financial information might be bad, we avoid looking at it. We don't check. We don't ask questions. We just keep teaching.

And to be fair, what were these teachers supposed to do? Ask the studio director every week if the company was solvent? The Guardian reports that bailiffs attended the Shoreditch studio in August. Cost-cutting measures changed the atmosphere. Teachers were told to use AI-generated music instead of Spotify. The signs were there, but they were easy to rationalise or miss when you love the community you work in.

That's the ostrich effect in a real setting. The information was uncomfortable, so it didn't fully register. Until it did.

## One small thing you can do this week

Look at your income for the last three months and notice how much of it came from a single source.

Not to panic. Just to see it. Because mental accounting loves a single bucket. When all the "reliable" money comes from one place, and that place disappears, there's no other bucket to draw from.

Just naming that, actually seeing the number, is the start of building a different picture in your head. Not a plan yet. Just clarity. What's that telling you?
